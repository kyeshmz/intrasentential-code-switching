1. Start with what the reader needs to know in {language}. Put actions in an order that makes sense. Introduce a file, place, or person before referring back to it.
2. Keep {language} as the grammatical base. Choose English phrases the audience might actually use and attach them using the surrounding language's own grammar. Mix phrase types, such as verb phrases, time expressions, and short clauses, instead of using only noun labels. Do not count a name, acronym, or isolated English word as a switch.
3. Keep the meaning exact. Preserve timing, quantities, conditions, and what each term refers to. Translate part of an English term only if the remainder is still a natural phrase and the instruction is unchanged.
4. Match the reader's relationship to the writer. Start with the task, skip filler, and repeat a useful term rather than renaming the same thing. Do not invent details or add fake mistakes to sound casual.
5. Before returning the draft, read it as the recipient. Check that the action and conditions are clear and that every sentence has a natural switch. If an English phrase sounds forced, move the switch rather than adding a throwaway phrase.
6. Vary sentence length and shape so the message does not read as a checklist. Let a sentence join two closely related points, give a brief reason, or follow on from the sentence before it.
